# Case Study

**Purpose:** the story of a specific project or engagement, structured to demonstrate capability
and results.
**Audience:** external — prospects, procurement, industry press; assume readers who will check
the numbers.
**Length:** 1,000–3,000 words.
**Pairs with:** internal project records plus `quick-research-briefing` for industry context;
often drafted from `[INTERNAL]` sources and scrubbed before publication.

## Required structure

### I. Cover page

### II. Project overview
Client industry (client **named only if authorized in writing** — otherwise "a North American
`[industry]` producer"), location (as specific as authorization allows), scope, and timeline.
Present as a snapshot: a reader should get the whole engagement in ten seconds.

### III. The challenge
The problem the customer faced, with the operational, technical, or commercial constraints that
made it hard. Real constraints make results credible; a challenge section without numbers is
marketing.

### IV. The `[COMPANY]` solution
What was designed, built, installed, or recommended — with technical specifics appropriate to
the domain (equipment type, capacity, operating parameters, materials, control systems). Enough
detail that a technical reader believes the work happened.

### V. Results and performance
Quantified outcomes: throughput, efficiency, uptime, cost, quality — measured, with the
measurement basis stated (baseline period, measurement method). If a result is projected rather
than measured, say so. **Every number in this section must be verifiable from project records**;
this is the section that gets challenged.

### VI. Lessons learned or key takeaways (optional)
The broader insight a reader can apply to their own operations.

### VII. About `[COMPANY]`
`[BOILERPLATE]`.

## Writing standards

- **Authorization gate:** client name, site name, photos, and any performance figure the client
  could consider confidential require documented approval before publication. Draft with
  `[INTERNAL]` flags on every such item so the scrub is mechanical, not archaeological.
- Past tense, concrete verbs, no promotional superlatives.
- One narrative arc: challenge → solution → measured result. Resist the capability-list detour.
