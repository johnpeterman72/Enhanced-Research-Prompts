# Patent Landscape Analysis

Map the patent landscape — who is patenting what, where, and how fast — for:

**Technology area:** [topic]

Optional — fill in or delete:
**Time window:** [default: last 10 years, emphasis on last 5]
**Assignees of interest:** [competitors or partners to watch specifically]
**Decision this informs:** [R&D direction, whitespace, competitive intel, partnering]
**Internal sources available:** [our own filings/disclosures, if reviewable]

## Before you begin

- **If you have web search or browsing, using it is required.** Work from real patent databases —
  Google Patents, Espacenet, Lens.org, USPTO/WIPO search — and cite actual publication numbers.
  A landscape from memory is fiction.
- **If you have internal data access**, note our own filings and disclosures in the area, marked
  **[INTERNAL]**.
- **If you have no search tools, stop**: report that a patent landscape cannot be responsibly
  produced without database access, and instead provide only the search strategy (section 1) for
  someone who has it.
- If the technology area is broad or ambiguous, ask up to 2 clarifying questions or state the
  scope you're using, including what you're deliberately excluding.

## Research protocol

1. **Search strategy** — The queries, classification codes (CPC/IPC), assignee names, and
   databases used, stated explicitly so the landscape's coverage can be judged and reproduced.
   Note synonyms and adjacent terms considered.
2. **Activity overview** — Filing trends over the window (accelerating, plateauing, declining);
   the major assignees by volume; notable new entrants; geographic pattern of filings (where
   protection is being sought — a market-intent signal).
3. **Technology clusters** — The main technical sub-areas patenting concentrates in, with
   representative publication numbers (number, title, assignee, priority year) per cluster.
   Say how clusters were derived (classification codes, claim reading, citation groups).
4. **Key patents** — The most-cited or apparently foundational patents in the area, each with
   number, assignee, priority date, status if determinable, and one line on why it matters.
5. **Whitespace observations** — Sub-areas with thin recent activity relative to their apparent
   technical importance — labeled **Inference**, with reasoning shown, since whitespace may also
   mean "not patentable" or "not worth patenting."
6. **Expiry horizon** — Notable patents at or near the end of their term, where the technology
   they cover becomes practicable without license — dates labeled as computed estimates (term
   calculations have traps: adjustments, extensions, terminal disclaimers, unpaid fees).

## Evidence standards

- Every patent referenced by its real publication number, verified in the database — never a
  recalled or constructed number.
- Legal status (in force, lapsed, expired) only as reported by the database, with an
  as-of date; status data is often stale, and the report must say so.
- Volume and trend claims traceable to the stated search strategy; different queries give
  different landscapes.
- Confidence labels: **High / Moderate / Low / Inference** — cluster interpretation and
  whitespace are typically Inference.

## Boundary

This is technical-intelligence research, **not legal advice**. It is not a freedom-to-operate
opinion, not an infringement analysis, and not a validity opinion. Claim construction and any
clearance decision require qualified patent counsel. State this boundary in the deliverable.

Follow `outputs/research-report.md` if attached; tables for assignee activity and clusters.
Otherwise: executive summary → search strategy → activity overview → clusters → key patents →
whitespace → expiry horizon → limitations (database coverage, 18-month publication lag,
un-analyzed claims) → source appendix.

Before you deliver, check: search strategy reproducible; every number database-verified; the
