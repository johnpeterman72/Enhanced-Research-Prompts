# SWOT One-Pager

**Purpose:** a fast, evidence-backed strategic snapshot of a company, product, technology, or
market position — one page, every entry defensible.
**Audience:** anyone aligning on strategy quickly; often an input to a workshop or a section of
a larger report.
**Length:** 1 page, hard limit.
**Pairs with:** `swot-analysis` (which does the research); also consumes output from
`competitive-landscape-analysis` and `market-research-analysis`.

## Required structure

### 1. Header
Subject, the **perspective** the SWOT is from (a SWOT of Company X *for* Company X reads
differently than one *for* a competitor evaluating it), as-of date, and capability disclosure.

### 2. The four quadrants
Presented as a 2×2 table or four titled lists: **Strengths / Weaknesses** (internal, present-day)
and **Opportunities / Threats** (external, forward-looking).

Rules for every entry:
- One line: the factor, then a parenthetical citation or **[INTERNAL]** flag, then a confidence
  label (**H / M / L / Inf**).
- Maximum 5–6 entries per quadrant — a SWOT with twelve strengths is a brainstorm, not an
  analysis.
- Internal/external discipline is enforced: if a "strength" describes the market rather than the
  subject, it's an opportunity, and vice versa.
- No entry without evidence. An uncited SWOT entry is an opinion wearing a framework.

### 3. So-what line (2–4 sentences)
The strategic read: which strength meets which opportunity (where to push), and which weakness
meets which threat (what could hurt most). This is the difference between a SWOT and a list.

### 4. Watch items (3–5 bullets)
The developments that would move an entry between quadrants or change its weight.

## Writing standards

- Specific beats general: "ISO 9001-certified fabrication with 15% cost advantage from in-house
  refractory work (source, 2025)" beats "strong manufacturing capability."
- Present tense for S/W, future-facing phrasing for O/T.
- If the evidence for a quadrant is thin, say so in the quadrant rather than padding it.
