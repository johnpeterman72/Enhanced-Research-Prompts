# White Paper

**Purpose:** long-form thought leadership that educates the reader on a technology, process,
industry trend, or engineering challenge, positioning `[COMPANY]` as a subject-matter authority
without reading as a sales pitch.
**Audience:** external — customers, industry peers, prospects; assume intelligent non-specialists.
**Length:** 2,000–6,000 words.
**Pairs with:** `comprehensive-research-analysis`, `engineering-technical-analysis`,
`market-research-analysis`, `technology-readiness-assessment`.

## Required structure

### I. Cover page
Title, subtitle, document type line, author/date band, confidentiality or copyright notice —
per the filled `style-guide-template.md` if provided.

### II. Executive summary (150–300 words, written last)
The problem or opportunity, the core argument, and the key takeaways. Stands alone.

### III. Introduction (10–15% of body word count)
Industry context or challenge; why this topic matters now; a brief roadmap of what follows.

### IV. Body sections (60–70% of body word count)
Logically sequenced sections with descriptive headings. Each section contains a clear topic
statement, supporting evidence or technical detail **with citations**, analysis and
interpretation, and a bridge to the next section. Organizational logic may be thematic,
chronological, problem/solution, or comparative — chosen for the argument at hand.

### V. Conclusion and implications (10–15% of body word count)
Restate the core argument in fresh language; summarize key findings; address practical
implications for the reader's operations. No new arguments or evidence.

### VI. About `[COMPANY]`
`[BOILERPLATE]` — standard company description and contact information. Omit if writing
unbranded.

### VII. References
All sources cited in the body: title, publisher, date, URL. A white paper built on research done
with these prompts inherits the source appendix from the underlying `research-report.md`;
single-source claims and estimates keep their flags in the authoring draft even if the labels
are edited down for publication.

## Writing standards

- Expert, direct, plain-spoken; a practitioner explaining something important to a peer.
- No promotional superlatives unless quoting a third party — let performance data speak.
- Data-bearing sentences carry numbers, not adjectives.
- Include a table of contents for documents over 2,000 words.
- One pull-quote-worthy "key insight" per 1,500–2,000 words; the sentence must appear verbatim
  in the body.
- Facts that came from research remain traceable: don't strip citations for aesthetics; move
  them to endnotes instead.
